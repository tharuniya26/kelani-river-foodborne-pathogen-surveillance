# Research background and question

Freshwater fish contact water and sediment through skin, mouth and gills, while the gut can reflect ingestion or residence. Site-specific culture detection can therefore describe different exposure interfaces, although it cannot by itself distinguish environmental deposition, handling contamination, or colonization. The original study compared presumptive *Staphylococcus*, *Salmonella* and *Shigella* detection across these four sites in 27 fish. This reanalysis asks how the **reported** site-specific frequencies differ and which conclusions remain supported by aggregate data.

A prior Kelani Basin water study found *Salmonella* in sampled surface and groundwater but reported no *Shigella* in those samples; its locations, period and matrices differ from this fish study, so it neither confirms nor refutes the fish observations. [Mahagamage, Pathirage and Manage (2020)](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/12/8/2187). Fish handling and adequate cooking matter for food safety, but this dataset does not measure illnesses or dose. The CDC describes staphylococcal food poisoning as toxin-mediated by *S. aureus*; a genus-level *Staphylococcus* finding is therefore insufficient to claim enterotoxin hazard. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/staph-food-poisoning/about/). The FDA describes staged culture and further confirmation for *Salmonella*, illustrating why colony appearance and a limited biochemical panel should be interpreted as presumptive identification. [FDA BAM](https://www.fda.gov/food/laboratory-methods-food/bam-chapter-5-salmonella).

The original contribution was specimen sampling, microbiological work and a conference abstract. The new contribution is source reconciliation, a reusable aggregate table, code, Wilson intervals and transparent figures. These analyses are exploratory descriptions, not a pre-registered hypothesis test or a second experimental study.
